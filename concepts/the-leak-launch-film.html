The idea

Every painting company in the country is bleeding money through channels it doesn't control, and calling that bleed "marketing." So the film opens in a world that has been drained — a blacked-out warehouse where forty paint buckets, each stenciled with a channel name, pour grey onto the floor. No color anywhere. That's the business before.

The payoff is literal for a painting company: the agents come online and color returns to the world. They seal the holes, chroma races up the walls, and the room ends up more alive than it has ever been. Nobody in the contractor-coaching space is making this. That's the point.

Four acts, one 15-second generation each, four takes batched per act. You keep the strongest of each and cut a 60-second film out of four clips.

No one is in this film

Version 1 put you on camera. This one doesn't, and it is the better film for it — every prompt below has been rewritten so there is no identifiable person in a single frame.

What you gain

Faces and hands are the two things AI video still gets wrong, and a face that shifts between Act I and Act IV destroys the whole illusion. Removing the cast removes the single largest failure risk in the production — no character training, no reference stills, no wardrobe continuity, no uncanny mouth on a talking head. Your regeneration rate drops hard.

What it costs you

You lose the trust that a real face buys, and you're selling one-on-one coaching. That has to come back somewhere, so it comes back in the voice.

Record the voiceover yourself

Do not use a synthetic voice on this one. Your voice becomes the only human thing in sixty seconds, which makes it carry more weight than it would over a talking head. Phone voice memo, quiet room, phone six inches off-axis from your mouth so you don't pop the P's. One take where you sound certain beats ten takes where you sound polished.

Where your face goes instead

This film builds the myth. It is not supposed to close anybody. Post a plain, unedited talking-head follow-up a day later — you, phone camera, no effects, explaining what you actually built. The contrast between the two posts does more than either alone, and it puts your face on the offer without putting it in the film.

The new continuity rule

With no cast, continuity is about the room, not a person. The same warehouse, the same bucket design, the same light direction, and the same lens language have to survive all four acts or it reads as four unrelated clips. The global tail below exists to enforce that — it goes on every prompt without exception.

Before you generate

One camera move per clip

15 seconds is long for a single generation. Give each clip one dominant move that runs the full duration and write the action as one continuous take. The phrase "continuous shot, no cuts" opens every prompt on purpose — asking for cuts inside one generation is what produces morphing.

Batch strategy

Your four slots per act are not four rolls of the same dice. Take A is the hero. B, C and D are genuinely different stagings, so when the hero fails you still have a usable act instead of four broken copies of one idea.

Build Act I first, then match to it

Generate Act I before anything else. Whichever take wins becomes your reference frame for the warehouse — feed that clip's last frame in as the start image for Act II so the geometry and light carry forward. Do the same down the chain. This is what replaces character consistency.

No text in frame, ever

Stenciled words on the buckets are the one exception and they will still come out imperfect — that's fine, they read as texture at speed. Everything else, every super and every number, goes on in post. Generated text always garbles.

Sound & post

Sound design

ACT I — sub drone at 40Hz, thick liquid pour, distant warehouse room tone. No music yet. Silence is the hook.

ACT II — paper storm, one reverse riser building the whole act, everything drops out for a half-second before the ember.

ACT III — the drop lands exactly on the slab ignition. Six hits, one per agent. Big low-end.

ACT IV — music pulls back to a single sustained pad so your voice sits on top. Never talk over a drop.

Cutting four clips into sixty seconds

Each act ends on a held frame and the next begins on movement, so straight cuts work — no transitions, no dissolves, no whip wipes. The only exception is Act II into Act III: hard cut to two frames of black on the ember, then the slab ignition. That black is the whole edit's punctuation.

If a generation drifts in the last two seconds, trim it and let the act run 13. A 56-second film is fine. A melting bucket is not.

Text treatment

Supers go on in post — condensed uppercase, bottom third, appearing on a beat and cutting out hard rather than fading. Two lines maximum. Every number gets its own screen. With no face on camera the supers are carrying more weight than usual, so give them room and let them hold.

The thumbnail

Pull it from Act III Take C — the frozen chromatic explosion around the single bucket. That frame is the most arresting image in the film and it still reads at 40 pixels wide in a feed, which is the only size that matters.

One caution before you post

The $47,915 and $124,000 are your real numbers and they're the strongest thing in this — but the second you put revenue on screen you invite screenshots. Make sure you're comfortable with those figures living publicly and permanently, because they will. If you'd rather not, Act III still lands hard on "six agents, one weekend" with no dollar figure at all.
